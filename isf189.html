Can reputation discipline the gig economy?

Experimental evidence from an online labor market, Amazon Mechanical Turk.

Alan Benson, Aaron Sojourner and Akhmed Umyarov. IZA Discussion Paper 9501, November 2015.

Read the paper

PDF

The problem: what if the employer just doesn't pay?

Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) was a website where people and companies posted small online jobs, like typing out what is in a photo or sorting comments into categories. Workers did them for a few cents each. The person posting the job is called a requester, and a job is called a HIT. [1, p. 5]

The worker does the job first. Then the requester picks one of two options. They can approve it and pay, or reject it. If they reject it, the worker gets nothing, the requester keeps the work, and there is no way to appeal. [1, p. 6]

A rejection also lowers the worker's approval rating, which is the share of their past work that got accepted. Many jobs require a high approval rating, so a few rejections can lock a worker out of work. [1, p. 6]

What the authors wanted to know

Because workers have no legal way to get paid, they have to rely on each other. Workers on MTurk share reviews of requesters online. The authors asked whether that kind of shared reputation actually works as a replacement for contracts and courts. [1, p. 4]

They split this into two questions.

  1. Do the reviews tell workers something true? Are requesters with good reviews really better to work for?
  2. Do workers actually avoid requesters with bad reviews, and does a good reputation help a requester get work done faster?

This matters beyond MTurk: Companies like Uber and Airbnb argue their rating systems keep drivers, hosts and customers honest, so they don't need the kind of licensing and inspections that older businesses face. [1, p. 2]

If ratings really can discipline people when the law isn't enforcing anything, that argument has some backbone. If they can't, regulators have a reason to step in. 

Research gaps

Most economists have argued for a long time that employers who break promises lose the trust of future workers, so a good reputation is worth protecting [8, pp. 3, 7 of 1][9, p. 8 of 1]. The idea shows up in most studies of sellers and firms in other markets [1, p. 8].

Most research on jobs looks the other direction: how employers figure out which workers are good [7, p. 9 of 1]. The question of how workers figure out which employers are good has gotten much less attention [1, p. 2].

The few studies on employer reputation have many weak spots. Some were lab games, which are not real jobs [2, pp. 8-9 of 1]. Others compared companies that already had good or bad reputations, like which firms get more job applicants [3, p. 9 of 1][4, p. 9 of 1], but those companies also differ in many other ways [1, p. 9].

A gap to be filled was a test in a real job market where someone changed an employer's reputation on purpose (dependent variable) and kept everything else the same. That is what this paper seeks to do [1, p. 4].

What workers could see, and Turkopticon

  • MTurk's job list showed the requester's name, the pay, how long you had and how many jobs were left. It did not say whether the requester usually pays, how long the job really takes, or how often they reject work. [1, p. 6]
  • So workers built Turkopticon, a free browser plugin. Workers who install it can rate requesters from 1 to 5. [1, pp. 6-7]
  • The four ratings are (1) communication, (2) pay for the time a job takes, (3) fairness in approving and rejecting, and  (4) how quickly they pay. [1, p. 7]
  • Scores under 2 show up red, 2 to 3 yellow and over 3 green. [1, p. 7]
  • By late 2013 it had 105,909 reviews from 8,734 workers about 23,031 requesters. [1, p. 7]
  • MTurk is a good place to study for this specific question. An argument that could be made for other studies limitations is that, for most jobs, you can't tell whether an employer behaves well because of a contract, the law or their own choice. On MTurk there is no contract and no appeal, so everything a requester does is their own decision. [1, p. 3]

Research design: two experiments

Experiment 1: do the reviews tell the truth?Experiment 2: does a good reputation help?
What it asksThe first experiment checks whether Turkopticon's ratings are accurate. It asks whether requesters with good ratings really pay more reliably and quickly than requesters with bad ratings.The second experiment asks whether a good reputation helps a requester get work done faster, when the job and the pay are otherwise the same.
How it worksTwo research assistants worked in tandem: one had the Turkopticon plugin and picked real jobs from requesters with good, bad or no ratings. The other did the jobs without the plugin, so that person could not see any ratings. [1, p. 10]The authors made 36 new requester accounts. On Turkopticon, they gave 12 of them good reviews, 12 bad reviews and 12 none. Each account posted the same $0.20 job for one hour, following a random schedule over three days. [1, pp. 12-13]
DataThe assistants completed 598 jobs. A program recorded the payments and rejections, to within a minute. The authors also collected Turkopticon ratings for 23,031 requesters to decide what counted as a good or bad rating (averaging out). [1, p. 10]The authors counted how many workers viewed, accepted and submitted the job each hour, which came to 1,641 submissions. They also posted a short survey asking 100 workers how they decide which jobs to take. [1, p. 17]
AnalysisThey compared the share of rejected jobs, the days until the requester decided, and the hourly wage across the three groups, using standard statistical tests. [1, p. 11]They used regression models built for counts to compare how fast work arrived for each type of requester, while accounting for the time of day and the day of the week. [1, pp. 14-15]

Experiment 1: are the reviews accurate?

Anyone can post a Turkopticon review, and nobody checks them. So the authors first tested whether the reviews were true. If you do a job for a requester with good reviews, do you really get treated better? [1, p. 10]

To find out, someone would have had to do many real jobs for requesters with good, bad and no reviews, and write down what happened. But if the person doing the job could see the reviews, they might work harder for the good requesters, and that could change whether the job gets approved. So two research assistants split the work, and only one of them could see the ratings. [1, pp. 10-11]

Because assistant 2 never saw the ratings, any difference in what happened next had to come from the requester, not from how hard the worker tried. [1, p. 11]

How each job was handled

1. A random list says which type comes next: good, bad or no ratings.
↓
2. Assistant 1, who can see the ratings, finds a real job from that type of requester.
↓
3. Assistant 1 sends the job to assistant 2, who can't see any ratings.
↓
4. Assistant 2 does the job as carefully as possible. A program records whether and when the requester pays.

What they measured for each job

  • Whether the job was accepted or rejected.
  • How many days the requester took to decide.
  • The hourly wage, which is the pay divided by how long the job took. **A rejected job counts as $0. [1, p. 11]

Experiment 1 results: the reviews were accurate

Green is requesters with good ratings (223 jobs), gray is no ratings (164 jobs), and red is bad ratings (211 jobs).

  • A worker who only took jobs from well-rated requesters would have made about $2.83 an hour. A worker who only took jobs from badly rated or unrated requesters would have made just under $2.00. That is about 40% more. [1, p. 12]
  • About one fifth of that 40% gap came from getting rejected and not paid. The other four fifths came from jobs taking longer than expected. [1, p. 12]
  • Good and bad requesters advertised about the same pay. Without reviews/the plug-in, a bad requester looks the same as a good one until the work is done. [1, p. 11]
  • The authors also checked that the ratings weren't just reflecting the type of job, like surveys versus image tasks. They found no link. [1, pp. 11-12]

*Rounded stats

Experiment 2: giving made-up requesters a reputation

Experiment 1 shows the ratings are accurate, but if and how  workers pay attention to them. To test that, the authors made up requesters from scratch, so they could decide what each one's reputation was more reliably. [1, p. 12]

Each fake requester had a common name, like Mark Kelly or Thomas Clark, that wasn't already rated high or low. The reviews were copied from real good and bad reviews on Turkopticon. For accuracy, one of the research assistants who saw only the text sorted 285 of the 288 reviews into the right group. [1, p. 13]

Every account posted the same job: workers read a picture of a grocery receipt and typed in the beers and other alcohol on it, with their prices. It paid $0.20. Each requester posted for just one hour and then disappeared. [1, p. 13]

Green is a requester with good reviews, gray is no reviews and red is bad reviews. Each row is one day and each square is one hour. [1, pp. 13, 36]

The order was random, but in every six-hour block each type posted exactly twice.

What a worker with the plugin saw

The authors also had three extra requesters post a 12 cent survey every hour, with reputations that never changed.  [1, p. 14]

Experiment 2 results: good reputations got work done faster

Each bar compares a type of requester with a bad-reputation requester, which counts as 1.0 (dashed line). A bar of 1.84 means 1.84 times as much work showed up (Table 4).

the paper's figure 4
  • Requesters with good reviews got about 84% more submissions than requesters with bad reviews. Requesters with no reviews got about 36% more. [1, p. 16]
  • The quality of the work didn't change: between 61% and 63% of submissions were error-free in every group. [1, p. 15]
  • Once workers opened a job and looked at it, they accepted it at about the same rate no matter who posted it. [1, p. 16]
  • Only about half of the workers surveyed (55 of 100) mentioned using Turkopticon. If more had used it, the effect would probably be bigger, by roughly 80% according to the authors. [1, p. 17]

How much more a bad requester would have to pay

To get the same number of workers as a requester with a good reputation paying $0.20, the authors estimate a requester with no reviews would need to pay about $0.37 and one with bad reviews about $0.59 [1, p. 16], using an estimate of how workers respond to pay from earlier research [10, p. 16 of 1].

Why would a requester pay at all?

The authors also wrote a small model to explain why a requester would pay when nobody can make them. [1, p. 18]

Suppose some workers can see whether a requester has ever refused to pay, and they will never work for that requester again. The other workers can't see that, so a requester who cheats keeps the money this time, but loses the workers who know, so future jobs get done more slowly. [1, p. 18]

The requester stays honest if what they would lose later is worth more than what they save now. In the model, that works out to δpy − w ≥ 0. In plain terms, the share of workers who can see reputations (p), how much the requester cares about the future (δ) and what the work is worth to them (y), multiplied together, have to come to more than the wage they would be skipping (w). [1, p. 19]

The point is that reputation is like a deposit. If enough workers can see it, losing it costs more than cheating gains.

Ethical considerations and limits

Ethical considerations

None.

Limits of what they found

  • In Experiment 1, the authors didn't assign reputations. Requesters with good ratings might also be more generous or more patient, so the 40% gap shows that ratings are accurate, not that reputation is casual. [1, p. 22]
  • It used one job on one website for three days. [1, p. 13]
  • Only about half of workers used the plugin. [1, p. 17]
  • Partway through, workers started talking about the experiment on Reddit, so the last 12 hours were left out of the main results. [1, pp. 14, 16]

Implications

  • For workers, shared reviews were useful. Following them was worth about $0.83 more per hour. [1, p. 12]
  • For employers, a good reputation worked like a higher wage. They could pay less and still get work done quickly. [1, p. 4]
  • The authors suggest that this studies results mean that, for platforms and policy, a rating system built by workers can partly stand in for contracts and enforcement. The authors suggest Amazon could publish each requester's average pay and rejection rate, and they point to OSHA's public announcements of workplace safety violations as a similar idea. [1, pp. 21, 23]

My caution is that this only works because workers volunteer honest reviews and enough of them use the tool. The paper also tests only short, simple jobs. For longer or riskier work, a reputation system might not be enough.

Would I use the same method?

No, but the my study and this one are related. This paper ran experiments inside a live job market and measured what workers actually did. My study is a online experiment/survey in social psychology and human-computer interaction (HCI). It looks at how interacting with AI affects people's support for egalitarian policy, and their attitudes and behaviors toward human workers.

The main correlation between the two studies is workers and the use of surveys/online experiments.

Why the methods fit (or don't fit) my study:

  • Yes: I can control exactly what each person sees and does, so the groups only differ in the part I change, which this study also did.
  • No: Support for a policy and attitudes toward workers are things people have to tell me, I can't see them from the outside the way you can see a job being approved or rejected.
  • No: AI models are unique in that they are not a static platform, and produce a variety of outputs, which their platform is not similar to.
  • Overall, I will probably not use most of this.


Questions I expect

Why use HTML?

Because I'm lazy. And a loser.

Couldn't bad requesters just make a new account?

Yes. When the study ran, a new account only took filling out some forms, however in July 2014 Amazon started requiring a legal name, an address and a tax ID number, which made new accounts harder to create. [1, p. 12]

How do we know the ratings weren't just about the type of job?

The authors checked this two ways: in Experiment 1, their categories for the jobs didn't line up with the ratings [1, pp. 11-12]. In Experiment 2, workers accepted the job at the same rate after previewing it, and spent the same time looking at it, no matter who posted it [1, pp. 16-17].

Only about half of workers use Turkopticon. What does that mean for the results?

The authors say the effect among workers who see the ratings is probably bigger, by roughly 80%. They also acknowledged that this paper can't be generalized for all workers. [1, p. 17]

Why didn't the quality of the work change?

Between 61% and 63% of submissions were error-free in every group, which the authors attribute to earlier work finding that higher pay also brought in more work but not better work. [1, p. 15]

Is $0.20 a realistic wage?

The job took about two minutes on average, between 113 and 136 seconds depending on the group [1, p. 15]. By my math, $0.20 comes to roughly $5 to $6 an hour. The paper cites a median reservation wage of $1.38 an hour for MTurk workers [1, pp. 5-6].

Was the model tested?

Not directly, the authors present it as consistent with their results rather than estimating it, but either way, they're not economists so shrug. [1, p. 5]

Does this apply outside MTurk?

Somewhat. [1, p. 22]

Why did they use a count regression?

They were counting how many workers showed up each hour, and the data didn't fit the basic version of the model, probably because of time-of-day effects, so they used a more flexible one. [1, p. 15]

Why would workers leave reviews at all?

"It takes time and can send competitors to the good jobs". The authors guess it could be to help other workers or to punish bad requesters, but they don't test it or cite anything relevant so. [1, p. 21]

How does this connect to your own study?

Both ask how information or experiences shape what people do around workers.
