welcome!

Hey! My name is Love Adu.

I study technology and society at UC Berkeley.

I used to be a member of the Board of Governors for the California Community Colleges (appointed by Governor Newsom), and I've done research and policy work at Haas, the Oxford Computational Political Science Group, OutVote, IRLE @ Berkeley, the California Department of Technology, Issue One, the Roosevelt Network, Groundwork Collaborative, and a few other places.

On the side I spend too much time dancing to early 2000s Beyoncé albums, thinking about the consequences of modern technology, and eating mango yogurt (shoutout Hui Lau Shan @ Berkeley).

Why Berkeley (or why not)some thoughts from a berk senioroctober 3rd, the day I decided to finally post on this damn site

Having the choice of choosing Berkeley is of course, relative to almost every other societal position one could hold, a great privilege.

However, if you are choosing between two great privileges, I could understand why it would be the question "Why Berkeley?" that plagues you.

Here are my reasons for Berkeley:

1. Transfer community

I would be lying and naive to say I chose Berkeley solely due to its transfer community — if it were my utmost priority, any of the CSUs admit more transfers per year than Berkeley or any of the UCs. However, relative to my other priorities (institutional prestige, opportunities, culture, location, etc.), Berkeley had a sufficient transfer community, and after having been on campus for a while, I can say I am quite satisfied with my experiences as a transfer. While nothing is perfect, living in Anchor House (the best apartment building on campus, dedicated solely to transfers) has provided me with a great deal of community and familiarity. I never felt discriminated against for being a transfer (though I'm sure if I was less strict on who I chose to be friends with, I probably would have been from some Bay Area charter school freshman or consulting club freaks), and never felt inadequate for being a transfer (though, why would I?).

2. Research community

Berkeley is among the best, if not the best, research institution in the country, and undoubtedly the best public one. The opportunities available for undergrads to engage with top-of-the-field research(ers) are truly unparalleled, and if you have any interest in pursuing research or academia, I would be remiss to encourage you to go elsewhere. Additionally, due to such a heavy research culture, lots of programs (like SSRP, Data Discovery, URAP, etc.) are either funded or well established, which brings stability and credibility to your experiences. Unless you have a hyper-niche field of study in which another institution is #1 for your field without doubt — Berkeley is your best bet.

3. Competitive culture

You are probably thinking, Love, what the fuck. And that's understandable; Berkeley's competitive culture has many, many negatives (see below in my "why not" section) — however, for a very certain type of student, it can be incredibly productive.

Personally, being surrounded by other hyper-ambitious people has been a great experience. I've been not only pushed to continue doing great work in great spaces, but to take pride in my accomplishments and never shy away from being exceptional. And while I've never been someone to hide my confidence in myself (see my case against unnecessary humility), it's incredibly freeing to be in a community where you won't be shamed, ostracized, or an outsider for that pursuit of excellence. If everyone around you is the shit, you'll be more comfortable feeling like you're the shit too.

4, 5, 6:

Berkeley is pretty great (or at least amongst the best it gets) if you're Queer, like public transit (free pass for the entire Bay Area!!!), and are Leftist and woke (Non-derogatory, non-gentrified).

my 'why not's:

1. Black community/being Black

I love the Black community at Berkeley — it has done wonders considering the small part of the student population we are — however, I would be remiss to not talk about exactly that.

If you are a Black/mixed person considering Berkeley, and having a robust Black population, Black professors, and Black culture (whether that be African, Black American, Caribbean, etc.), is a priority for you — you will not get that here, at least not at all comparable to lots of other universities, let alone HBCUs. I mean — Berkeley is 4% Black, lmao.

I grew up abroad, in a non-Black country as a mixed person, and thus do not feel as great an absence of culture as I would if I were raised in a Black community. I cannot say that if you at all care about having that community off-rip, and not having to find and build it, that you'll like Berkeley very much.

Additionally, Berkeley students are quite racist. That's a claim I'm willing to make — see my Daily Cal article on this. Pre-professional societies that aren't designated for Black students will almost always have some level of racism or, at best, no Black people (which is arguably a result of racism as well, but I digress). It's up to you to decide if that's something you want to fight against or tolerate, or avoid altogether.

Overall, I think Cal is a great place for students, but maybe not Black ones. Hopefully that changes soon.

2. Housing

While I have not had much issue in this department, I'd be negligent not to mention it. Housing in Berkeley sucks, as it does in most of the Bay. I'd search Reddit for more info from better sources than I.

3. Competitiveness (the other side)

I've noticed a great deal of my peers, especially transfers, have imposter syndrome, or small fish in a big pond syndrome. And while I'm not here to disuade you in those thoughts, because I don't know you or your accomplishments relative to the Berkeley student population, I can say that it is not at all productive. Unfortunately, Berkeley doesn't help in dissolving those feelings from a large portion of its student populace.

Berkeley's pursuit of exceptional people and excellence broadly has a major problem, in which it often lacks nuance, compassion, and understanding. The culture that demands excellence asks not from where one started, but where one is now, and judges from there.

This, of course, like many other "meritorious" judgments of achievements, is highly problematic when you drop even an ounce of real-world context into the fold. Not everyone has the same access to educational opportunities — to judge on an assumed baseline and not within context will always reproduce inequality. And while some may argue it's not academia's job to fix societal inequality — as people who study it, it certainly should be our job to not reproduce it. You cannot treat equality as a virtue — treat everyone equally in an unequal world, and then expect equality to magically appear. Equity is needed to reach any semblance of equality. It's quite a simple concept.

Berkeley does not seem to grasp it very well.

This is seen in the massive push against equity measures, the general negative sentiment around transfers, affirmative action, equity programs, club application culture, etc. I cannot praise the pursuit of excellence while ignoring the backs of students who are stepped on in that path.

the end

Wow, that was long. And yet, it still is not comprehensive of all the pros and all of the cons of Berkeley. If you want to talk more about why or why not Berkeley, feel free to reach out.

To be (apply to PhDs) or not to be (die)My pre-PhD application justification for pursuing a PhD, academia, and the broader intellectual hellscape broadly.october 3rd, 2026 (my 19th birthday)

Over the course of my undergraduate experience, I have always known I wanted to pursue a PhD. And another degree, at some point. Audacious, I know.

Nonetheless, my dual degree pursuits have swayed lots — I took the LSAT my freshman year, considered public policy (shout out Harvard PPLC for dissuading me of that notion), and even, though now a relic of a distant past, more immature Love, an international policy degree at Tsinghua.

While I would be a sadist to make anyone sit through my reasoning for wanting those degrees, I know my reasoning for a PhD to be much more stable, rational, though unfortunately, equally as annoying and pretentious. Here is some of that reasoning, both for the readers' (your) pleasure, and to remind myself, if I one day find myself in need of such motivation, why I started this process in the first place.

DISCLAIMER:

Somewhere through writing this, I had the realization that I absolutely come off as someone who believes they know everything about everything. I don't — not at all. All of these thoughts are based on my brief experiences in various fields, advice or mentorship from people in said fields, and introspection. Definitely don't take this as advice — I come from a great deal of privilege in which my main concerns do not have anything to do with the job market. Do not compare yourself to me!

1. I want to exist in academia

Simply put, I highly doubt you'll ever see me work a corporate job. However woeful, I view working in public organizations, whether that be civil society or academia, to be my passion, the only pursuit worth pursuing in my purview.

That's not to say I view academia as a virtue free from sin — academia has massive problems in a variety of contexts (I'm sure you'll see me speak on many of them on this blog at some point soon), but I also view the academy as a necessary function of society — particularly my intended study of society and it's many forms. Who are we, if not understanding ourselves as we? The production of knowledge for humanity (whether directly in production of work to the field, science communication, educating the next generation of academics, so on and so forth), to me, is the highest pursuit I can achieve, and also one I have proven to be the most decent at thus far. I would not fare well as a corporate lawyer in NYC or SF, or in manual labour, or probably any other aspect of the labour market, and one, if particularly functionalist, could argue it is my duty to pursue what I am best at, for it is my duty to contribute the best I can to society.

My best is, most likely, academia. Or its offshoots.

(This is also a really long way of saying I don't want to get a job post-grad).

2. I love research

While this may seem redundant to the point above, said point never said anything about loving academia in its functions — just that I view it as a worthy pursuit and that I've done well in it so far.

It's quite lucky that I not only find worth in academia, but also in its requirements.

I like designing studies, defining DVs and IVs, communicating my research, conversing with other academics, policymakers, and the public, love hitting run on my scripts, love finding correlations in a dataset that took me two tries to get API access for, love submitting IRB exemption paperwork to be denied and resubmit nonexempt, and a lux myriad of other tasks required by this profession. I love research, and I love being able to have some level of ownership over the work I produce, in ways I wouldn't otherwise in say, corporate or policy work.

And while I'm certainly not so arrogant to assume I know everything out the ins and outs of being an academic when I am far removed from being considered one — I come from a family of academics, and a strong network of mentors that have given me the privileges to understand, at least more than one would assume a measly undergrad such as myself would know, about being an academic.

If their experiences are anything to go off of, the actual day-to-day functions aren't going to be what turns me away from being an academic.

3. I would be bored elsewhere, I think.

Academia is the constant pursuit of knowledge as an objective, and I don't feel that corporate or policy work would satisfy that for me whatsoever. In my experiences in other fields outside academia, I have frequently been (rightfully) critiqued to "stay on objective" and leave the deeper work in a topic to the academics. I often found myself diving deeper into the citations that we used for briefs, but never got to expand on those works. I wager that if I continued in a path outside of academia, I would find myself staring soullessly outside my 14th-floor high-rise San Francisco financial district cubicle, in devastatingly grey business casual, wondering what my life would be like if I had pursued academia instead of a line of work where I would be docked on a quarterly review for going "off objective" for pursuing my curiosity. Academia would allow me that pursuit of curiosity.

4. I said I was going to get a PhD for 4 years (pride).

Not too much elaboration on this one, lol.

The end!
